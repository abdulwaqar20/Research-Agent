--- FINAL ANSWER ---
CRISPR gene editing works by turning a bacterial immune system into a programmable “molecular scissors” that can cut DNA at any chosen location and let the cell’s own repair machinery rewrite the genome.

**Key steps**

1. **Design of a guide RNA (gRNA)** – A short synthetic RNA is created that is complementary to the exact DNA sequence the researcher wants to edit. The gRNA forms a complex with the Cas9 protein and directs it to that target site【S1】.  
2. **Cas9 binding and cleavage** – The Cas9‑gRNA complex scans the genome, binds where the gRNA matches, and the Cas9 nuclease opens both strands of DNA, creating a double‑strand break (DSB) at the precise location【S1】.  
3. **Cellular DNA‑repair pathways** – Once the DSB is made, the cell repairs it by one of two natural mechanisms:  
   * **Non‑homologous end joining (NHEJ)** – An error‑prone process that often inserts or deletes a few nucleotides, typically disrupting the target gene.  
   * **Homology‑directed repair (HDR)** – A high‑fidelity pathway that can use an externally supplied DNA template to insert, replace, or correct specific sequences (knock‑in).  
4. **Resulting genome edit** – By exploiting NHEJ or HDR, scientists can delete, deactivate, or precisely modify genes in living cells, enabling targeted genetic engineering for research, medicine, agriculture, and more【S1】.

**In short:** A custom gRNA guides Cas9 to a chosen DNA stretch, Cas9 cuts the DNA, and the cell’s repair systems—either the sloppy NHEJ or the precise HDR—rewrite the genome according to the researcher’s intent.
